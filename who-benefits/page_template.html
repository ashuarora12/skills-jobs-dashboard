Who benefits? Health, jobs & gender in the World Bank Group portfolio

Can health investment create jobs, especially for women, and does labour mobility play a part? Before asking whether it works, it helps to ask what is measured. This dashboard uses the World Bank Group's own FY25 results data to ask three questions. Who is being reached? Does the portfolio measure the health–jobs–mobility link? Do projects deliver to women as planned?

02

Does the portfolio measure the health–jobs–mobility link?

A small evidence-extraction exercise, in the spirit of tools like the World Bank's ImpactAI. Every project's development objective and results indicators are read by a transparent keyword classifier. It tags six themes and keeps the exact phrase that triggered each tag as evidence.

For each theme, a random sample of tagged projects was checked by hand to measure precision: how many of the tags are correct. The first version failed that check for two themes and was fixed; both versions' scores are shown.

03

Which projects fall behind, and can we predict it?

An early-warning model, the kind of tool a team could use to decide where to adapt. For each result a project reports, it compares the share of the target delivered with the share of the implementation period that has passed. The result is flagged behind if delivery trails time elapsed by more than 10 percentage points. Results less than 20% of the way through implementation are too early to judge and are excluded.

Two models predict that flag from project features: region, income group, fragility, sector, instrument, size and timing. They're scored on out-of-fold predictions, with all of a project's results kept in the same fold so the model never sees a project it's being tested on.

04

Are women reached as planned, and does fragility widen the gap?

For results reported both in total and for women, compare women's planned share of beneficiaries with their achieved share. A negative gap means women are falling behind plan.

Then ask whether projects in fragile and conflict-affected settings (FCS) show a larger gap than otherwise similar projects elsewhere. This uses propensity-score matching, the same quasi-experimental approach used in impact evaluations when randomisation isn't possible.

Step 1 · Which women's figures are actually measured?

Before measuring a gap, check whether one can exist. Many women's figures in the Scorecard are not counted directly. They're derived by applying a fixed share, such as the female share of the population, to the total. When the same share is applied to the target and to progress, the planned and achieved shares are identical by construction.

Step 3 · Matched comparison: FCS vs. similar non-FCS results

FCS projects differ from others in ways that could affect the gap on their own: more are in Africa and in low-income countries. Each FCS result is matched to its nearest non-FCS neighbour on the estimated probability of being in an FCS setting. That probability is based on region, income group, project size, result type, status and the planned share of women. The plot shows covariate balance before and after matching.

05

Methods, limitations & responsible data

What each number is, where it comes from, and what it cannot tell you.

Data provenance

  • Results and project records: World Bank Group Scorecard exports downloaded from scorecard.worldbank.org, FY25 cycle, as of 30 June 2025. Covers people reached with health, nutrition & population services; WASH; gender-equality and economic-opportunity actions; financial services; and health-emergency capacity.
  • Context: Scorecard vision indicators for poverty at $3.00/day, food and nutrition insecurity, and basic hygiene. The latest year is shown for each region.
  • Region codes: the export's “ACW” row is All countries. The seven regional rows sum to it exactly, and the pipeline checks this.
  • Filters applied: WBG-wide organisation rows; the all-projects disability flag (the “Yes” rows are a subset and would double-count); results the export marks as double-counted are excluded; exact duplicate rows across the four exports are dropped.

What is sourced vs. estimated

Sourced Numbers taken directly from Scorecard exports, or simple arithmetic on them (sums, shares, % of target).

Model estimate Outputs of models fitted in this analysis: predicted probabilities, importance scores, matched differences. Each comes with its uncertainty or validation.

Check reporting Figures that are internally inconsistent in the source and probably reflect reporting practice, not reality.

Nothing on this page is illustrative or simulated. Where data is missing, the page says “no data”.

Limitations

  • Matching is not randomisation. Propensity-score matching only balances what is observed. Unobserved differences between FCS and non-FCS projects, such as implementation capacity or security, could still bias the comparison. Balance on three covariates stays slightly above 0.1 after matching (section 04). The result is a matched association, not a causal effect.
  • Small informative sample. Only results where women are counted directly can show a gap, which leaves a few hundred results. The confidence interval is correspondingly wide.
  • “Behind” uses a straight-line benchmark. Many results are back-loaded, so young projects look behind more often (section 03). The model learns this, but the flag is a screening signal, not a verdict on performance.
  • The text classifier is rule-based, and recall is unmeasured. Precision was checked on small samples (5–12 per theme). Projects that describe a theme in words the rules don't anticipate will be missed.
  • Coverage. The Scorecard reports corporate results indicators, not every indicator a project tracks.

Privacy, fairness & reproducibility

  • Privacy: the analysis uses only public, aggregated, project-level data. No individual or household records are used or stored.
  • Fairness: model performance is reported by region, income group and FCS status (section 03). The subgroup where it is weakest is named, not hidden.
  • Validation: the classifier's review samples, labels and the failing first version are saved in the repository. The review is a draft pending the author's confirmation.
  • Reproducibility: one script (analysis/who-benefits/pipeline.py) rebuilds every number from the raw exports with fixed random seeds. Code and data on GitHub →
